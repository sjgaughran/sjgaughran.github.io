Research

Our lab uses an integrative framework to study the origins and maintenance of biodiversity. We connect evolutionary history, population structure, and ecological context to ask how species diversify, persist, and respond to environmental change. We combine molecular tools, comparative datasets, and collaborative field-based research.

The evolution of deleterious variation

How do harmful mutations arise, persist, and influence the evolution of natural populations? We investigate how mutation, natural selection, genetic drift, and demographic history shape deleterious genetic variation. By combining evolutionary genomics with cell culture experiments, pedigree estimation, and samples collected across generations, we connect patterns in genomes to the processes that produce them. A central question is why populations differ in their burden of deleterious variation—and what those differences mean for their persistence.

Molecular adaptations in marine mammals

Marine mammals have evolved remarkable solutions to life in the ocean, including prolonged breath-holding, tolerance of low oxygen, and survival in extreme environments. We study the genetic changes underlying these adaptations, with a particular focus on seals. Comparative and population genomic approaches allow us to investigate how natural selection shapes genes involved in physiology, immunity, and reproduction, and how different lineages arrive at similar—or distinct—evolutionary solutions.

Evolutionary conservation genomics

How can evolutionary history help us make better conservation decisions? We use genomic data from contemporary populations and museum specimens to reconstruct changes in population size, connectivity, and genetic variation through time. Our work asks how long-term demographic history and recent human impacts shape the genetic challenges facing threatened species. We aim to place present-day diversity in its historical context and inform strategies that preserve both populations and their capacity to evolve.

Species delimitation and systematics

Understanding biodiversity requires understanding where species boundaries lie and how those boundaries arise. We use genomic data and statistical models to investigate relationships among populations and species, particularly in groups with recent divergence or histories of hybridization. Our work on Galápagos giant tortoises explores how different sources of evidence reveal distinct evolutionary lineages. By incorporating museum specimens and methods suited to limited genomic data, we also bring rare and historical samples into studies of diversification.
